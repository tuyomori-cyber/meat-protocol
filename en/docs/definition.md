# What is Meat Protocol?

Meat Protocol is an informal name for workflows in which a human forms part of an information or action path between systems. The “meat” is the human body doing the relay; the name is playful, while the observation is about interaction design.

It does not treat human involvement as a failure. It asks which human roles are present and whether part of their work can plausibly be separated into mechanical work.

## Human roles

| Role | Question | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Selection | What is the target? | “Compare these three files.” |
| Routing | Where should it go? | choose the relevant project |
| Transform | How should it change? | format or redact a payload |
| Judgment | What does it mean? | assess whether an error matters |
| Approval | Should it happen? | approve sharing or execution |
| Transport | How is it carried? | copy a path into a chat box |

## Candidate test

A Meat Protocol candidate is a workflow where a human forms part of an information or action path between systems, and at least part of that role could plausibly be separated into mechanical work.

Ask: **Are you actually making a new decision every time you do this?** Repetition can turn a prior decision into a rule, context, or metadata, leaving mechanical human work behind.

## Classification and automation

Classification and automation are separate questions. For each role, consider whether it is technically automatable, valuable to automate, safe to automate, or intentionally human. Transport is a common candidate, but routing, transformation, judgment, and approval may also be automated in the right conditions.

## What can be carried?

- content, such as a copied response;
- a reference, such as a local path, URL, or item identifier;
- state, such as an error, selection, or application context; or
- reconstructed context, such as decisions re-explained after switching sessions.

Not every screenshot, summary, or upload is a problem. They may embody useful selection, redaction, explanation, or consent. Classify the workflow and its separable mechanics—not the human judgment around it.
